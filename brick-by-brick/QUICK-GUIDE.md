# Quick guide

**Gather the facts, lay them in courses, and let the past show the pattern it
was built in.**

You lay one fact per line. Every other panel is a view of the same wall, rising
as you type.

---

## The first course

Five minutes. Everything after this is levelled off it.

1. **Open the page.** A board of panels and an empty Pallet. For something to
   practise on, open the Pallet's **☰** menu and press **Sample** — it loads a
   real pile, and none of it is yours, so handle it roughly.
2. **Find The Pallet.** The panel full of text. That is the whole of your
   research; every other panel is a view of it.
3. **Change a line.** Watch the rest of the board follow. Nothing to save,
   nothing to refresh.
4. **Click a name in Bricks.** The board narrows to every record that names
   them. A family here is not a tree — it is everyone who turns up in the same
   documents.
5. **Add a panel.** The **🧰** tab on the right-hand edge. Find **Promenade**
   and press its **+**: it is not on the board when you start, and you would
   never find it otherwise.
6. **Press Export.** Before you close the tab. *Saving* explains why.

When you are ready to bring your own, The Pallet's **☰** menu takes a paste or a
whole folder.

## Choosing your tools

**A board is a bench, not a building.** Put out the panels you are using and put
the rest away. Four are left off the bench entirely, because they are no use
until you have records to look at:

> **Promenade**, **Theme**, **Map Style** and **Map Editor** are not on the
> board when you first open the page. You have to add them.

**The 🧰 tab** on the right-hand edge holds everything not currently out. It
shrinks as you take tools out and fills as you put them back, and each entry
says what that panel is for.

| To do this | Do that |
|---|---|
| Add a panel | Press its **+**. It drops into the first free space. |
| Put it somewhere exact | Drag the entry out of the list and onto the board. |
| Change its size | The two number boxes on its entry — width × height, in cells. |
| Move one | Drag it by the **✥** handle in its top-left corner. |
| Swap two | Drag one onto another of the same size. |
| Make it bigger | The **+** buttons on its edges. Only the ones that can't collide appear. |
| Take one off | The **×** in its corner. |
| Start over | **reset board**, in the 🧰 panel. |

**Putting a tool away never loses work.** Your bricks live in The Pallet, not in
the panel you closed. Take one off, put it back, and it returns exactly as you
left it, still scrolled where you were. Rearranging cannot break anything.

Two more controls at the top of the 🧰 panel: **Columns** for how wide the board
is, **Row px** for how tall a row sits.

## What a brick is

One line. One thing you know.

```
+|👤|<george-isles>|George Isles|Powerloom Tender
```

| | |
|---|---|
| `+` | **A document says so.** Use `-` if you're guessing. |
| `👤` | It's a person. There are symbols for places, dates and documents too. |
| `<george-isles>` | A nickname you invent, so you can point at him later. |
| `George Isles` | His name. |
| `Powerloom Tender` | Anything else worth keeping. |

You never have to type this by hand. **Brick Builder** has a button for each
part and lays the line for you.

### Brick and mortar

A bricklayer mixes mortar *softer* than the brick on purpose. When the ground
shifts it is the mortar that cracks, and mortar can be raked out and repointed
without touching a single brick.

Your `+` records are the brick: what a document actually says, and it does not
change. Your `-` inferences are the mortar — they hold the wall together, and
they are the part you expect to redo when a better record turns up. Keep the two
visibly apart and you can repoint later instead of demolishing.

Six months from now you will not remember which was which.

## What each tool does

Every panel has a **help** link in its corner that opens its own write-up. In
short:

**Laying** — *The Pallet* holds every brick. *Brick Builder* lays them for you.
*Gazetteer* is where places sit on the ground; paste coordinates straight from
Google Maps.

**Checking the work** — *Bricks* lists everyone and everywhere, and a dotted
name is someone you have mentioned but never set down, which is usually your
next lead. *Red Flags* catches what is out of true. *Classification* shows how
each line was read. *Tally* counts. *Notation reference* lists the symbols.

**Standing back** — *Timeline* puts every dated record in order, one row per
person, with worked-out dates drawn hollow. *Map Viewer* puts your places on the
ground. *Document* shows one record big enough to read the handwriting.
*Promenade* walks your people through the places their records put them.

**Your own hand** — *Filter* narrows every panel at once. *Theme* is eight
colours. *Map Style* and *Map Editor* are the map's own look, and your own
additions to it.

## Saving

**Read this part twice.**

Your bricks live in this browser, not on a server. Nothing is uploaded and
nobody else can see it. That privacy has a price:

> **Clearing your browsing data will delete your work.** So will a browser that
> tidies up after itself, and so will opening the page on another computer.

So **press Export**. It writes an ordinary text file onto your computer, and
that file is the only copy that is truly yours. Export again whenever you have
laid a good stretch of wall.

The line at the top always says where things stand. Red means unsaved changes.

## When it doesn't sit true

| | |
|---|---|
| A line isn't showing up | Look at it in *Classification*. Red usually means a missing `\|`. |
| Someone's appeared twice | Two nicknames for one person. Pick one, use it everywhere. |
| A name is dotted | Mentioned, never set down. Not an error — a lead. |
| The map is blank | Places need coordinates. Add them in *Gazetteer*. |
| The map is blue and empty | Map pictures blocked. Running it yourself? It needs **port 3000**. |
| Everything's vanished | Check *Filter*. Something is still switched on from earlier. |

## Four things the trade knows

**No one hands you the plan.** Headings starting with `#` become your groupings
— a family, a village, a theory you are testing. The page uses your categories
and never imposes its own.

**Keep the wrong records.** Mark it `-`, say why you doubt it, and leave it in.
A contradiction you have written down is research; one you have deleted is a
hole you will fall into again next year.

**Strength comes from the overlap.** Line every vertical joint up and you have a
stack, not a wall — every bond that exists, English, Flemish, stretcher, exists
so that a joint never sits directly above the joint below it. Research holds
together the same way. One record saying a thing is a stack; three records from
different sources naming the same person is a bond. That is what links and the
map's connector lines are for: not decoration, but the overlap that carries the
weight.

**Nothing is locked away.** Your whole pile is plain text — readable in any
editor, on any computer, in twenty years, with or without this page.

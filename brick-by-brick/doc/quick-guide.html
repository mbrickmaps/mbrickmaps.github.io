The first course

Five minutes. Everything after this is levelled off it.

  1. Open the page. A board of panels, with a small example pile already in it. None of it is yours yet, so handle it roughly.
  2. Find The Pallet. The panel full of text. That is the whole of your research; every other panel is a view of it.
  3. Change a line. Watch the rest of the board follow. Nothing to save, nothing to refresh.
  4. Click a name in Bricks. The board narrows to every record that names them. A family here is not a tree — it is everyone who turns up in the same documents.
  5. Add a panel. The 🧰 tab on the right-hand edge. Find Promenade and press its +: it is not on the board when you start, and you would never find it otherwise — how panels work.
  6. Press Export. Before you close the tab. Saving explains why.

When you are ready to bring your own, The Pallet's ☰ menu takes a paste or a whole folder.

Choosing your tools

A board is a bench, not a building. Put out the panels you are using and put the rest away. Four are left off the bench entirely, because they are no use until you have records to look at.

Promenade, Theme, Map Style and Map Editor are not on the board when you first open the page. You have to add them.

The 🧰 tab on the right-hand edge holds everything not currently out. It shrinks as you take tools out and fills as you put them back, and each entry says what that panel is for.

You never have to type this by hand. Brick Builder has a button for each part and lays the line for you.

Brick and mortar

A bricklayer mixes mortar softer than the brick on purpose. When the ground shifts it is the mortar that cracks, and mortar can be raked out and repointed without touching a single brick.

Your + records are the brick: what a document actually says, and it does not change. Your − inferences are the mortar — they hold the wall together, and they are the part you expect to redo when a better record turns up. Keep the two visibly apart and you can repoint later instead of demolishing.

Six months from now you will not remember which was which.

What each tool does

Every panel has a help link in its corner that opens its own write-up. In short:

Laying

The Pallet holds every brick. Brick Builder lays them for you. Gazetteer is where places sit on the ground — paste coordinates straight from Google Maps.

Checking the work

Bricks lists everyone and everywhere, and a dotted name is someone you have mentioned but never set down — usually your next lead. Red Flags catches what is out of true. Classification shows how each line was read. Tally counts. Notation reference lists the symbols.

Standing back

Timeline puts every dated record in order, one row per person, with worked-out dates drawn hollow. Map Viewer puts your places on the ground. Document shows one record big enough to read the handwriting. Promenade walks your people through the places their records put them.

Your own hand

Filter narrows every panel at once. Theme is eight colours. Map Style and Map Editor are the map's own look, and your own additions to it.

Saving

Read this part twice.

Your bricks live in this browser, not on a server. Nothing is uploaded and nobody else can see it. That privacy has a price:

Clearing your browsing data will delete your work. So will a browser that tidies up after itself, and so will opening the page on another computer.

So press Export. It writes an ordinary text file onto your computer, and that file is the only copy that is truly yours. Export again whenever you have laid a good stretch of wall.

The line at the top always says where things stand. Red means unsaved changes.

Four things the trade knows

No one hands you the plan

Headings starting with # become your groupings — a family, a village, a theory you are testing. The page uses your categories and never imposes its own.

Keep the wrong records

Mark it −, say why you doubt it, and leave it in. A contradiction you have written down is research; one you have deleted is a hole you will fall into again next year.

Strength comes from the overlap

Line every vertical joint up and you have a stack, not a wall. Every bond that exists — English, Flemish, stretcher — exists so that a joint never sits directly above the joint below it.

Research holds together the same way. One record saying a thing is a stack; three records from different sources naming the same person is a bond. That is what links and the map's connector lines are for: not decoration, but the overlap that carries the weight.

Nothing is locked away

Your whole pile is plain text — readable in any editor, on any computer, in twenty years, with or without this page.

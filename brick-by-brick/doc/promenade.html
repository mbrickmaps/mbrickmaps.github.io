The Promenade

Your people, walking the ground their records put them on. And how to dress them.

What it shows

A side-on scene you walk along. Every declared person is one figure; every place with coordinates is a column with a signpost over it.

The horizontal axis is longitude, west on the left, the same order you would meet these parishes crossing Perthshire. A person stands among the places their records actually name. Someone recorded at both Perth and Scone stands between the two.

A record that names a person and a place puts that person there. That is the same rule the map's connector lines and the event markers use. The two can never disagree about who was where.

The page never guesses where somebody lived. If no record places a man, he stands in The Promenade, the column at the end holding whoever the named places leave over. Not knowing is a result, and the street shows it as one.

You can guess as much as you like. Make a column for a suspected family or for everyone in one lawsuit and drag people into it. Put a place in the gazetteer at 0, 0 for a household that sits nowhere on a map. The page attaches no meaning to either.

Your guesses are kept apart from the evidence. They live in brick.json with your placements and never touch the pile, because which column a man stands in is your thinking and no document says it.

Street and grid

Street

Geography. Distance between two figures means something, columns sit where the parishes sit, and everyone paces about where they stood. This is the view for where.

Grid

An isotype board, one figure per person, ranked and counted. Distance means nothing. You can sort it however you like. This is the view for how many, and when.

Switching between them walks everyone across. Each figure keeps its position and travels to the new one. You can follow a particular person across and see where they landed.

Sorting only appears in the grid. On the street the order is the geography. Offering to re-sort it would be offering to move Perth.

Placing people

Each place is a column. Drop somebody anywhere inside one and they belong to that column.

Pick a figure up and they lift out of the line. Dotted frames appear over every column, full height, and the one you are over brightens. Nobody should have to guess which frame is about to catch them.

Let go and they fall to the ground, sliding sideways into the middle of their column on the way down. You are not placing a figure at a coordinate; you are putting a person in a group. The x you released at only decides which.

Columns grow. A place holding ten people is wider than one holding two, and everything east of it shifts along. Roughly 110px for one or two, 160 for five, 270 for ten.

The pen

The pen is a dotted box centered over the street. Nobody starts in it. Drag somebody there to say you have decided you cannot place them.

It does not scroll. It stays put while the street slides past behind it, the same distance from every column you might want to drop someone into. When it is empty it shuts to a small dotted tab captioned "no place recorded", and it opens again while you are carrying somebody.

They stand about in a loose huddle rather than a grid, because a grid would say these people have an order and nothing is known about them yet. Drag someone back into the pen to say you no longer know where they were.

None of this writes to your bricks. No brick, no coordinate, no gazetteer entry. Placing someone is how you have chosen to look at your records, not a claim about them.

Finding

One box under the pen, placeholder find a brickling or a place. It searches people and places at once.

The two used to be separate fields, and choosing between them meant knowing which kind of answer you wanted before you knew what was there. Now one word does both. Matching people light up and the rest of the scene dims. Matching places stay as columns and the others go, so dun leaves Dunblane, Dunkeld, Dunning, Little Dunkeld and Dunbarney. Five columns instead of fifty-nine.

It searches everyone on the panel, not only the unplaced, by name and by uid. Nobody is removed. Placements survive it: filter a column out of view and the people pinned there fall back to The Promenade rather than vanishing.

The box only appears when somebody is in the pen. Since nobody starts there, you will normally not see it.

Click a figure to single them out. The rest of the scene dims and their parishes stay lit. Click a signpost for the reverse: that place, and everyone recorded there. Click bare ground to clear.

Saving

Placements and column colors persist in the browser, and travel as a file.

Every drop is written to browser storage immediately. An arrangement survives closing the tab. That storage is this browser on this machine. It does not follow you elsewhere, and clearing site data takes it. So does Clear all in the page header, which names placements and column colors among the things it removes.

For anything more durable, save hands you a brick.json:

{
  "brickByBrick": 1,
  "saved": "2026-08-29T17:11:03.366Z",
  "promenade": { "pins": [["robert-moncur", "perth"], …] },
  "columns":   { "tints": [["perth", { "from": "#5b2c6f",
                                       "to":   "#c05a2a",
                                       "alpha": 0.6 }], …] }
}

Drop it in your research folder beside bricks/ and it is read automatically whenever you open that folder. The merge log reports how many placements and colors it found. load takes one from anywhere.

It is deliberately not your bricks. A brick is what a document says; this is how you have chosen to look at them. Delete it and you lose an arrangement, never a record.

Coloring a column

Click a signpost and a bar appears naming that place. It carries two gradient stops, an opacity slider, a ribbon color for group columns, two lines of your own text, then ↺ and ×. ↺ clears the color. × only closes the bar.

The gradient fills that column's sky and stops at the horizon. The ground is one continuous plane that everybody stands on. Cutting it into colored stretches would make neighbours look like they stood on different surfaces.

That is the whole system. The file's name is what decides who gets it. There is nothing to configure and no list to keep in step. Edit default.png itself and you change everyone, or everywhere, at once.

Two folders rather than one because the names would collide otherwise: a parish called Mason and a mason are different pictures.

Place scenes

Named for the place's uid, not its display name. Use places/little-dunkeld.png. Drawn centered in its column, standing on the ground line, behind the people. Scaled down to fit the column and never blown up past its own pixels. It grows with the column as people are added.

A place with no picture of its own falls back to images/places/default.png, a two-storey red-brick house with a slate roof and four windows. Nothing tints or fades it. It says "a building" without claiming to be any particular one.

Delete that file and places without art show nothing at all: no frame, no placeholder, no gap. That is a perfectly good way to run it. A bare street reads as "no scenery yet" rather than as fifty identical cottages, and you may prefer it.

Draw it 102 px wide. A column is 110 px until a third person joins it, The page fits art to the column less 8 and never enlarges it past its own pixels, so 102 is the widest that still draws 1:1. Wider is allowed and simply scales down in a quiet column, which softens the pixels.

Height is free: the scene is anchored at the bottom. It grows upward. Copy places/default.png for the scale of a person, then enlarge the canvas. At 52 px it draws well short of the 102 px a column allows.

Who wears what

One sheet per wearer. Four frames across, four rows down, and the rows are what a figure is made of, from the skin outwards:

          frame 1   frame 2   frame 3   frame 4
row 1     body      ·         ·         ·
row 2     bottom    ·         ·         ·
row 3     top       ·         ·         ·
row 4     hat       ·         ·         ·

The filename says who it is for, and that is the entire configuration:

images/wear/<who>.png     who = a uid, a trade, or "default"

mill-worker.png           every mill worker
mill-worker-96x96.png     the same thing. A trailing -WxH is
                          dimensions, not part of the name
george-isles.png          him and nobody else
default.<row>.png       everyone else

Labeling a sheet with its own size is worth doing and costs nothing: the first thing you need when you reopen one is what to set the canvas to. The page strips a trailing -WxH before matching, but only for art in an opened research folder. A file committed to images/ is requested by its exact slug, so mill-worker-96x96.png sitting in the repository is never found. It never changes who the file is for.

Rows are independent. Leave a row transparent and it falls through to the next sheet in the chain: the person's, then their trade's, then default, then the four rows drawn in code.

Two things sit ahead of all of that. Drawings the Sprite Editor has pushed into browser storage are checked first, then an opened research folder, then images/. And within one wearer, a loose <who>.<row>.png beats that same wearer's combined sheet, being the more specific statement of the two. A sheet with only a hat drawn on it changes only the hat, and the wearer keeps their trade's coat.

Redraw the body row and that person becomes a different figure entirely. No flag, no setting, no table. Whatever distinction you want to draw for, draw it.

Layers share an origin. A garment may use any part of the cell: a brim wider than the head, or a tool out in the prop margin. Nothing is clipped to a row. A row gives a position in the stack. You do not draw inside a box.

The grid comes from the image, and bigger means bigger. Four frames across and four rows down, always. A 96 × 96 sheet is 24 × 24 cells and a 160 × 128 sheet is 40 × 32. A larger cell is not scaled back down: the scene's cell grows to the largest thing worn and the garment overhangs, which is what makes a greatcoat look like a greatcoat rather than a coat at higher resolution.

Everything registers the only two ways that survive a change of size: centered horizontally, and standing on the ground line. People in oversized clothes and people in ordinary ones share a street quite happily, because the page works the cell out per person.

How a trade is recognized

The trades half of the table is keyed on the trade as slugged from the first clause of the detail field:

It stops at the first comma, semicolon, full stop or bracket, and gives up past 28 characters rather than inventing a filename out of a sentence. No file means the ordinary figure. You can draw one uniform or none and nothing breaks either way.

Marking a drawing

Mark pixels in the Sprite Editor and the drawing tells the page where its windows, door, chimneys and signpost are.

There are four marks. Use the flag tools, paint over the pixels you mean, and the picture looks exactly the same afterwards: the mark is stored in the alpha of pixels you already drew. 255 is opaque, 254 is a hair less opaque, and nothing can see the difference except the code that reads it back.

MarkAlphaWhat it does
▣ glass254These panes light at night. The flame tints the pane rather than filling it, so dark astragals over pale glass stay dark.
▯ door253The way into this place. The region becomes a target you can knock on.
☁ smoke251Every marked pixel is a chimney and puffs rise from it.
⚑ anchor252Where this place's name stands. One per drawing.

PNG is lossless and never touches the alpha you wrote, so the marks travel with the file. Copy it, mail it, commit it. There is nothing to keep in step and nothing to lose. Around a dozen more flags fit below 251 before a marked pixel would start to look faded.

The anchor is the post's foot

A marked pixel is where the signpost stands, not how high it climbs. The post runs from the underside of the board down to that pixel and stops. Mark a roof and the sign is bolted to the roof; mark the ground and it is planted in the street; mark nothing and it keeps the standard height every other sign has.

Dragging the board up lengthens the post. Dragging it down to its foot leaves none of it, which is what a sign lying against a wall looks like. The mark itself never moves with the drag: it is a fixed point in the picture.

An anchor can go in open sky. Every other flag needs a drawn pixel to ride in, and empty space has none. The anchor is written the other way up when it lands on nothing, alpha 3 rather than 252: as invisible on nothing as the others are on something. Only the anchor gets this. Glass, a door and a chimney are properties of drawn pixels and mean nothing hanging in the air.

Smoke

Each marked pixel is one chimney with its own drift, and the puffs rise behind the building rather than over it. A puff is cut off horizontally at the line of its own chimney, so smoke never appears below the pot it came out of.

The smoke bar sets colour and amount. At night, and as the sun drops, it takes the light with everything else.

Doors

A marked door lights under the pointer. Click and it knocks: you hear it, and the door flexes. Every press raps, so two presses are two raps, and the second one opens the place.

Knock repeatedly and it gets louder. The step from the first rap to the second is the large one, since two presses are the gesture anyone actually makes; later raps taper toward a ceiling. A second of quiet resets it.

Inside is a reading of who that place holds, in two tabs. Who lists the people, what puts them there, and anyone visiting from elsewhere. When lays the same place out as everything that happened there, in order.

A door is a view and never an edit. Nothing you do behind one reaches the pile.

The sky

Scenery, not data. Nothing above the roofline says anything about your research.

The sun is the light

Drag the sun and the whole scene answers. High up it is yellow-white over a pale sky; as it falls it runs through orange to a deep red at the horizon, and the ground, the buildings, the figures and the smoke all take that colour with it. It is an environmental light rather than a lamp: nothing is lit from a direction, everything is tinted by the hour.

The ground draws in front of the sun, so it sets behind the street rather than over it. Switching to night gives you the same arrangement after dark, which makes the two ends of the dial dawn and dusk.

The moon sits just above the background, behind the buildings, and holds still while the street pans. It fades as it nears the horizon, and turns whiter and more transparent toward dawn.

Clouds

Each cloud is one silhouette baked from several overlapping lobes, so nothing shows through the joins. Three dials on the scenery bar:

numberNothing at all at one end, an overcast at the other.
sizeA haze of specks up to weather the size of the street.
distanceDown, they travel with the buildings and might as well be painted on them. In the middle they hold still while the street goes past, the way the sun does. Past that they cross the other way, which reads as weather moving while you walk.

Clouds wrap in screen space rather than world space, so panning never runs you off the end of the band into an empty sky. They drift with the wind on top of whatever the street is doing.

Colors are yours

A pixel is the color you painted it. The page recolors nothing by era or by marker, and there is no reserved palette to avoid.

Each wearer's sheet does get a small deterministic nudge, seeded from their uid, so twenty millworkers in the same coat do not read as twenty copies. Hue shifts by up to 14° and lightness by up to 0.05. A color you chose to identify a trade therefore varies a little between the people wearing it.

The status line

The line under the street is this panel's status bar, and says only what is true at the time:

14 bricklings · 9 placed by their records · 6 signposts ·
showing everyone at Killin, click again to clear ·
filtered to 12 of 59 bricks · 60 fps

Selecting somebody dims everyone unrelated and is the panel's own state; it changes nothing about the pile. The board filter does change what the panel can see. The two are worded apart so one is never mistaken for the other.

Reduced-motion stops figures travelling between arrangements, and stops the idle timer restarting the drift. It does not stop the walk cycle, the pacing, or a drift you started yourself.

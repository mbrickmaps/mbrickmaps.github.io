What it shows

A side-on scene you walk along. Every declared person is one figure; every place with coordinates is a column with a signpost over it.

The horizontal axis is longitude — west on the left, the same order you would meet these parishes crossing Perthshire. A person stands among the places their records actually name, so someone recorded at both Perth and Scone stands between the two.

A record that names a person and a place puts that person there. That is the same rule the map's connector lines and the event markers use, so the two can never disagree about who was where.

Nothing here is invented. A person no record places does not get parked somewhere plausible — they wait in the pen, and stay there until you say otherwise. "We don't know where he was" is a finding, not a gap to fill.

Street and grid

Street

Geography. Distance between two figures means something, columns sit where the parishes sit, and everyone paces about where they stood. This is the view for where.

Grid

An isotype board — one figure per person, ranked and counted. Distance means nothing, so it can be sorted however you like. This is the view for how many, and when.

Switching between them is a walk, not a cut: everyone keeps their position and travels to the new one, so you can follow a particular person across and see where they landed.

Sorting only appears in the grid. On the street the order is the geography — offering to re-sort it would be offering to move Perth.

Placing people

Each place is a column. Drop somebody anywhere inside one and they belong to that column.

Pick a figure up and they lift out of the line. Dotted frames appear over every column, full height, and the one you are over brightens — nobody should have to guess which frame is about to catch them.

Let go and they fall to the ground, sliding sideways into the middle of their column on the way down. You are not placing a figure at a coordinate; you are putting a person in a group. The x you released at only decides which.

Columns grow. A place holding ten people is wider than one holding two, and everything east of it shifts along. Roughly 110px for one or two, 160 for five, 270 for ten.

The pen

People with no recorded place wait in a dotted box centered over the street. It doesn't scroll — it stays put while the street slides past behind it, so it is the same distance from every column you might want to drop someone into.

They stand about in a loose huddle rather than a grid, because a grid would say these people have an order and nothing is known about them yet. Drag someone back into the pen to say you no longer know where they were.

None of this writes to your bricks. No brick, no coordinate, no gazetteer entry. Placing someone is how you have chosen to look at your records, not a claim about them.

Finding

Two boxes, stacked under the pen, doing deliberately different jobs.

BoxSearchesDoes
find someone unplaced Only people in the pen, by name or uid Lights matches and names them; dims the rest. Removes nobody, so you can still see how many you are choosing from.
filter to a place Place names and uids Narrows the street. dun leaves Dunblane, Dunkeld, Dunning, Little Dunkeld and Dunbarney — five columns instead of fifty-nine.

Someone already standing in a column can be found by looking at the column, which is why the first box ignores them. Placements survive the second: anyone pinned to a filtered-out column waits in the pen and returns when you clear the box.

Click a figure to single them out — the rest of the scene dims and their parishes stay lit. Click a signpost for the reverse: that place, and everyone recorded there. Click bare ground to clear.

Saving

Placements and column colors persist in the browser, and travel as a file.

Every drop is written to browser storage immediately, so an arrangement survives closing the tab. That storage is this browser on this machine — it does not follow you elsewhere, and clearing site data takes it. So does Clear all in the page header, which names placements and column colors among the things it removes.

For anything more durable, save hands you a brick.json:

{
  "brickByBrick": 1,
  "saved": "2026-08-29T17:11:03.366Z",
  "promenade": { "pins": [["robert-moncur", "perth"], …] },
  "columns":   { "tints": [["perth", { "from": "#5b2c6f",
                                       "to":   "#c05a2a",
                                       "alpha": 0.6 }], …] }
}

Drop it in your research folder beside bricks/ and it is read automatically whenever you open that folder — the merge log reports how many placements and colors it found. load takes one from anywhere.

It is deliberately not your bricks. A brick is what a document says; this is how you have chosen to look at them. Delete it and you lose an arrangement, never a record.

Coloring a column

Click a signpost and a bar appears naming that place, with two gradient stops, an opacity slider and a ×. The gradient fills that column's sky and stops at the horizon — the ground is one continuous plane that everybody stands on, and cutting it into colored stretches would make neighbors look like they were standing on different surfaces.

That is the whole system. The file's name is what decides who gets it, so there is nothing to configure and no list to keep in step. Edit default.png itself and you change everyone, or everywhere, at once.

Two folders rather than one because the names would collide otherwise: a parish called Mason and a mason are different pictures.

Place scenes

Named for the place's uid, not its display name — places/little-dunkeld.png. Drawn centered in its column, standing on the ground line, behind the people. Scaled down to fit the column and never blown up past its own pixels, so it grows with the column as people are added.

A place with no picture of its own falls back to images/places/default.png — a low wall and a tree, muted so the people in front of it still read. It is deliberately generic: it says "somewhere" without claiming to be anywhere.

Delete that file and places without art show nothing at all: no frame, no placeholder, no gap. That is a perfectly good way to run it — a bare street reads as "no scenery yet" rather than as fifty identical cottages, and you may prefer it.

Draw it 102 px wide. A column is 110 px until a third person joins it, and art is fitted to the column less 8 and never enlarged past its own pixels — so 102 is the widest that still draws 1:1. Wider is allowed and simply scales down in a quiet column, which softens the pixels.

Height is free: the scene is anchored at the bottom, so it grows upward. Copy places/default.png — it is already the right size, and the wall and tree give you the scale of a person before you start.

Who wears what

One sheet per wearer. Four frames across, four rows down, and the rows are what a figure is made of, from the skin outwards:

          frame 1   frame 2   frame 3   frame 4
row 1     body      ·         ·         ·
row 2     bottom    ·         ·         ·
row 3     top       ·         ·         ·
row 4     hat       ·         ·         ·

The filename says who it is for, and that is the entire configuration:

images/wear/<who>.png     who = a uid, a trade, or "default"

mill-worker.png           every mill worker
mill-worker-96x96.png     the same thing — a trailing -WxH is
                          dimensions, not part of the name
george-isles.png          him and nobody else
default.png               everyone else

Labeling a sheet with its own size is worth doing and costs nothing: the first thing you need when you reopen one is what to set the canvas to. The page strips a trailing -WxH before matching, so it never changes who the file is for.

Rows are independent. Leave a row transparent and it falls through to the next sheet in the chain — the person's, then their trade's, then default, then the built-in. A sheet with only a hat drawn on it changes only the hat, and the wearer keeps their trade's coat.

Redraw the body row and that person is a different figure entirely — no flag, no setting, no table. Whatever distinction you want to draw for, draw it.

Layers share an origin, so a garment may use any part of the cell — a brim wider than the head, a coat past the knee, a tool out in the prop margin. Nothing is clipped to a row; a row is a position in the stack, not a box you draw inside.

The grid comes from the image, and bigger means bigger. Four frames across and four rows down, always — so a 96 × 96 sheet is 24 × 24 cells and a 160 × 128 sheet is 40 × 32. A larger cell is not scaled back down: the scene's cell grows to the largest thing worn and the garment overhangs, which is what makes a greatcoat look like a greatcoat rather than a coat at higher resolution.

Everything registers the only two ways that survive a change of size: centered horizontally, and standing on the ground line. People in oversized clothes and people in ordinary ones share a street quite happily — the cell is worked out per person.

How a trade is recognized

The trades half of the table is keyed on the trade as slugged from the first clause of the detail field:

Brick detailTrade key
Powerloom Tenderpowerloom-tender
Farmer, immigrated to Utah in 1852farmer
Assistant Registrar (Perth)assistant-registrar
Mill worker; later a cartermill-worker

It stops at the first comma, semicolon, full stop or bracket, and gives up past 28 characters rather than inventing a filename out of a sentence. No file means the ordinary figure — so you can draw one uniform, or none, and nothing breaks either way.

Key colors

Not colors so much as slots. When a garment is loaded, every pixel is checked once: an exact match on one of these three is repainted in that wearer's color, and everything else passes through as you drew it — an apron, a hod, a bonnet, a pair of boots.

This is what lets one garment serve every century. Paint the parts that should age in key colors; paint the parts that identify the trade in real colors, and they survive every recoloring.

#ff00ffCoat — takes the era color
#00ffffLegs and arms — takes the era trim
#ffff00Hat — removed altogether after 1970

Keep the keys in a garment and it still ages: it goes through the same recoloring as the ordinary figure, taking its wearer's era palette and a small nudge seeded from their uid. Twenty millworkers read as millworkers without being twenty identical sprites.

Paint a part in a fixed color instead and it stays that color for everyone — which is exactly what you want for the thing that identifies the trade.

Start from the default itself. images/wear/default.png is not a copy of the default figure kept beside the docs — it is the default, the file the page actually loads. Body, bottom, top and hat, key colors already in place. Copy it to images/wear/<who>.png and edit; clearing a row hands that row back to whatever is behind it.

They are ordinary PNGs in the repository. Open one, draw, save. Nothing regenerates them and nothing overwrites them.

Delete them and nothing breaks: the same four rows are drawn in code as a last resort, so a copy with no art still has everybody dressed. There is no "install the art" step.

Delete it and nothing breaks: the same four rows are generated in code as a last resort, so a page opened from file:// or a copy with no art still has everybody dressed. There is no "install the art" step.

Exact means exact. The match is on the three byte values with no tolerance, so a soft edge, a feathered brush, a partial opacity or a JPEG round-trip leaves pixels that are nearly magenta — and those never swap. Draw the key areas with a hard pencil at 100% opacity, and save as PNG.

A hat layer you want kept after 1970 simply has no yellow in it. Yellow means "this is the hat, take it off in the modern band".

The default body and all three default garments are generated in code against this same geometry, so you can replace one slot, all of them, or none. There is no "install the art" step.

Era bands

Which palette a person wears, taken from the middle of their life.

Someone born in 1840 who died in 1901 is dressed for the 1870s, because that is where they spent their adulthood — not for the decade they were born in.

BandMidpoint beforeHat
georgian1845tall
victorian1890tall
edwardian1925bowler
interwar1970cap
modern—none

These are loose on purpose. Nobody changed coat on the stroke of 1890, and a parish register rarely gives you a date worth hanging a decade on. The years exist so the same person always dresses the same way, not as a claim about costume history.

Someone with no dates at all gets victorian, not modern — a parish corpus is overwhelmingly nineteenth century, and putting an undated man in a windcheater is the louder claim.
